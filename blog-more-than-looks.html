Guide · Why a website · November 2026

A good website isn't about looks. It's about the phone ringing.

The reasons trades give me for not having a proper website, a job I lost to a missed call, the numbers on slow sites, and what Google says it actually looks at.

What trades tell me

I've known and worked with a lot of tradies over the years. These are the reasons I hear most for not having a proper website, and what I say back.

“I get all my work from word of mouth.”

This is the first thing most tradies tell me. But people who get recommended to you still look you up before going ahead. If they find nothing, or something that looks abandoned, they go with the next name that builds their trust more.

A site with before and after photos, and an About page showing how experienced you are and your certifications: that's the missing link to more customers.

“I'm already busy. I don't want more work right now.”

A site also lets you pick better jobs and charge properly, instead of taking whatever comes. Would you work less for the same return? Of course you would. Scalability is the key to a successful business.

“I get leads from lead sites and Facebook.”

Those platforms are rented. Lead sites charge you per lead or a membership fee, and list you right next to your competitors. A Facebook page is free, but Facebook decides who sees your posts, and reaching more people means paying for ads. You own your business, so owning the data that comes with it is the best option.

“It's too expensive.”

A working website for a one-person or small operation shouldn't cost thousands. One or two extra jobs covers it.

“I've tried a website before.”

Unfortunately, most designers build simply for looks. Slow sites, no call button, never linked to your Google Business Profile properly, and not built to what Google looks for. Google is weighing up how far it can trust your business, and every box you tick counts.

The job I lost to a missed call

When I was driving, I called a customer back after a job. He didn't pick up. Then he called me, and I missed it. I called back, and he didn't pick up again. In the end I had to send a message. When I followed up the next day, he replied: it was an urgent moving job, and he'd gone with someone else.

He had already chosen me. He wanted to book. If I'd had a website where he could put all his details in straight away, and I could see them as they came in, that would most likely have been a closed job.

With a website, you get all the information right away, even when the phone isn't picked up. Once your site grows, you can even add your own work calendar, so customers can book the job and pay a deposit themselves.

This could happen to you again. And you wouldn't even know if that customer could have been a lead for future jobs, because you've got none of his details saved.

Ask yourself this. When you want a service, you want the quickest, at the price you want to pay. If you'd followed up, could that customer have come back to you regularly? And then recommended you to others, bringing in even more word of mouth from there.

For anything digital, the first step is a site that's technically sound and meets the basic conditions Google's algorithm looks for.

Why a website comes first

A website is the foundation of your digital setup. Other companies have one. Are they more skilled than you? Can they do the job quicker? Maybe, maybe not. But if you're not competing online at all, in a world that's almost all online, customers will pick someone else before they even know you exist.

Nobody is asking you to change the way you work. A website takes some of the admin and customer questions off your plate. If you pick one thing, make it a website, even before social media. It links to your Google Business Profile.

How does a customer know you're a registered business? How can they decide to go ahead if you haven't shown yourself clearly, somewhere they can find you? Build that trust up front, and when you speak to them it's just about booking the job. An FAQ page answers their common questions before they even ring.

That's the time you save. As they say, time is money, and nobody likes wasting either when there's a quicker way.

The Google figure comes from 2016, when phones were slower. The point still stands: a slow page loses people before they see your work. The site we built for Granite City Cars went from 48 to 98 on Google's mobile speed test.

What Google actually looks at

Google is the biggest search engine in the world. It tells local businesses that its results are based mainly on three things:

  • Relevance: how well your business matches what someone searched for. Google asks for complete, detailed business info. A page for each service you do, and the areas you cover, helps here.
  • Distance: how far you are from the person searching. You can't change where you're based, but you can tell Google every area you cover.
  • Prominence: how well known you are. Google counts links and mentions of you across the web, and how many reviews you have and your score.

On top of that, Google's guidance on content names four things it looks for: experience, expertise, authority and trust. It says trust matters most. Your before and after photos, your certifications, your real name and address, and your reviews are how you show it.

Google also measures how a page feels on a phone: how fast the main content shows (under 2.5 seconds is good), how quickly it reacts when tapped, and whether things jump around while it loads.

The bottom line

If you do high-quality work for your customers, your website should be built to the same standard. To grow a business, you have to spend on it.
